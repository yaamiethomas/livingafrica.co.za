ABOUT LIVING AFRICA

Twenty-five years, one land base.

How a small Stellenbosch investment club became a diversified property and energy group on Johannesburg's East Rand.

Living Africa started long before the name existed. In 1999, a group of ten friends from Stellenbosch University pooled savings into an informal investment club — Struct Capital — buying small residential properties around Johannesburg on the side of ordinary jobs. It was a way to learn the market, one modest deal at a time.

THE INTRODUCTION THAT SET THE DIRECTION

The turning point came in 2008. An introduction to Michael Jordaan Sr., trustee of the Wits Gold Mining Realisation Trust, opened access to a very different kind of opportunity: former mining land on the East Rand, held by DRDGold and related entities, much of it still carrying mine dumps and awaiting rehabilitation. Land that was cheap, unglamorous, and largely overlooked by the rest of the property market — but sitting directly in the path of Johannesburg's eastward growth.

Living Africa began assembling that land through direct purchases and long-dated options, betting on patience: buy at mining-land prices, hold through the rehabilitation cycle, then develop or sell as the land became usable and municipal demand caught up. An option acquired over 220 hectares of DRDGold land in 2008, for a strike price of R1 million, became the platform the group is still developing today — split, over time, into residential townships, a cemetery site, and what is now a 200MW+ solar programme.

A PARTNERSHIP BUILT ON DAILY CONTACT

Graham Ransom joined the business in 2011, initially on the commercial side, and became a director and shareholder within a few years. What started as a working relationship became the operating core of the group: multiple calls a day, a clear split between deal structuring and admin on one side and on-the-ground execution on the other, and two decades — between them — of East Rand relationships. James travels to Johannesburg roughly every two weeks; the rest is run remotely from KwaZulu-Natal.

PROVEN, REPEATEDLY

The model has been tested across market cycles: land bought from mining companies and business rescue processes, held, and sold to municipalities or developed into residential stands, at multiples of the original cost. Comet Village, Delmore Park, Matholesville — each followed the same pattern before Reiger Park, Farrar Park and Withok took it further. Along the way the group also learned what not to repeat: a high-effort, low-margin apartment and office development at Simbithi (The Verve) taught a lasting lesson about weighing time and operational risk against return, not just the return itself.

WHERE THE PLATFORM IS TODAY

The same land base now supports four parallel workstreams: municipal land sales and settlements, residential township development, utility-scale solar on the Ekurhuleni PPA programme, and income-producing property. A trust structure sits above the operating companies; project-specific SPVs sit below them; and a Mauritius holding structure supports the solar platform's international partners. It is, in substance, the same discipline the founders applied in 1999 — know the land, know the numbers, hold your nerve — applied at a very different scale.
